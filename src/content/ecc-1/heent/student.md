# POCUS Module 2 — HEENT: Ocular, Neck & Vascular

This module is part of the PHSU Ultrasound Integration Initiative and provides required preparatory content for hands-on ultrasound instruction.

Covers the "HEENT" objectives.

**Estimated completion time:** 45–60 minutes.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, the student will be able to:

- Evaluate the eye globe and its internal components for ocular pathology.¹⁵
- Differentiate vein from artery using the compression technique.¹⁵
- Identify the sonographic neck layers and evaluate cervical lymph nodes.¹⁴⁵
- Identify the internal carotid, external carotid, and vertebral arteries.¹³⁵
- Identify the thyroid and adjacent structures (internal jugular, trachea, esophagus).¹⁴⁵⁶

## Quick Reference Guide

This section is intended as a bedside reference during the hands-on scanning session.

### Probe & Preset

- **Probe:** Linear high-frequency transducer for neck and eye.¹⁵
- **Preset:** Ocular preset (low output) for the eye; small-parts/vascular preset for the neck.⁵

### Key Views / Structures

- Ocular: transverse and sagittal over the closed eyelid.¹⁵
- Transverse neck at the thyroid level (thyroid lobes, carotid, IJV, trachea, esophagus).³⁴⁵⁶
- Vein-vs-artery compression test (IJV vs. common carotid).¹⁵

### Normal Findings

- Globe: anechoic vitreous, smooth retina, mobile optic nerve sheath posteriorly.¹
- Thyroid: homogeneous, mid-level echogenicity; trachea shows air comet/ring-down.⁴⁵⁶
- IJV is compressible, larger, and varies with respiration; carotid is round, pulsatile, non-compressible.¹
- Normal lymph node: oval with an echogenic fatty hilum.¹⁴

### Common Pathology / Key Artifacts

- Retinal detachment: tethered hyperechoic membrane; vitreous hemorrhage: low-level mobile echoes.¹
- Suspicious node: rounded, loss of fatty hilum, hypoechoic.⁴
- Non-compressible vein → thrombus (see DVT in MSK/vascular).¹

### Image Optimization Tips

- **Depth:** Shallow depth (3–4 cm) for the superficial neck and eye.⁵
- **Gain:** On the eye, reduce gain and output (ocular preset) to satisfy ALARA; flood with gel.⁵

### Common Pitfalls

- Applying pressure on the globe — never compress the eye.¹
- Mistaking artery for vein — always confirm with compression ± color Doppler.¹
- Under-gaining the vitreous can hide a subtle detachment or hemorrhage.¹

### Clinical Pearl of the Day

On the eye, less is more — flood with gel and never compress the globe.

## Self-Assessment

**1. How do you distinguish the internal jugular vein (IJV) from the carotid artery at the bedside?**\
A) The carotid is compressible and oval-shaped; the IJV is round and pulsatile\
B) The IJV is compressible, non-pulsatile, and varies in size with respiration/Valsalva; the carotid is round, pulsatile, and non-compressible\
C) Both vessels are equally compressible, so color Doppler is the only reliable method\
D) The IJV lies medial to the carotid and is smaller in all patients

*Correct: B — Why: The IJV is a low-pressure venous structure that collapses with gentle probe pressure and changes size with respiration/Valsalva, while the carotid is a high-pressure arterial structure that stays round, pulsates, and resists compression.*

**2. What ocular preset/output change should you make before scanning the eye, and why?**\
A) Increase gain and depth to penetrate the globe, since the eye is a deep structure\
B) Switch to a low-output ocular preset to satisfy ALARA and limit acoustic energy to the eye\
C) Use the abdominal preset since it has the widest field of view\
D) No preset change is needed; any preset is safe for ocular imaging

*Correct: B — Why: The eye is a delicate, superficial structure sensitive to thermal and mechanical bioeffects, so the ocular preset lowers output in line with the ALARA principle (As Low As Reasonably Achievable).*

**3. Which structures would you expect to identify together in a single transverse view at the level of the thyroid?**\
A) Thyroid lobes, common carotid artery, internal jugular vein, trachea, and esophagus\
B) Globe, lens, and optic nerve sheath\
C) Parotid gland, mandible, and masseter muscle\
D) Thyroid isthmus and epiglottis only

*Correct: A — Why: A transverse neck view at the thyroid level shows the thyroid lobes centrally, the carotid and IJV laterally on each side, the trachea centrally/posteriorly, and the esophagus typically just left of the trachea.*

**4. Which transducer (probe) is most appropriate for HEENT applications such as the eye and neck?**\
A) Curvilinear low-frequency probe, for deep penetration\
B) Linear high-frequency probe, for superior resolution of superficial structures\
C) Phased array probe, for its small footprint\
D) Endocavitary probe, for its narrow field of view

*Correct: B — Why: Higher frequency sacrifices depth penetration but provides much better resolution, which is the right tradeoff for superficial structures like the eye and neck.*

**5. What scanning depth is generally appropriate for superficial neck and eye structures?**\
A) Shallow depth (roughly 3–4 cm), since these structures are superficial\
B) Deep setting (15–20 cm), similar to abdominal scanning\
C) Depth setting doesn't matter for these exams\
D) Maximum available depth, to increase resolution

*Correct: A — Why: Setting the depth just past the structure of interest keeps it magnified onscreen and maximizes resolution; excess depth shrinks the target and wastes image detail.*
